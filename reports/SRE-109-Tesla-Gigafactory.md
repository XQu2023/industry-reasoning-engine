## 1. Executive Summary

Tesla stated a goal of producing a mass-market electric car in approximately three years and disclosed a plan to build a large-scale factory — the Gigafactory — with strategic battery manufacturing partners, aimed at cutting lithium-ion battery cost through scale, innovative manufacturing, less logistics waste, co-located processes, and lower overhead [facts 1–3, 10]. Tesla states the factory is designed to reduce cell costs faster than the status quo and, by 2020, to produce more lithium-ion batteries annually than were produced worldwide in 2013; by the end of the first year of mass-market / Gen III volume production, Tesla expects pack cost per kWh to fall by more than 30% [facts 4, 5, 17]. The same-day 10-K describes integrating battery precursor material, cell, module, and pack production in one location, with site still unchosen among Arizona, Nevada, New Mexico, or Texas; full capacity targeted for 2020 sufficient for about 500,000 vehicles annually plus stationary storage; and CapEx of $4–5 billion through 2020, of which about $2 billion from Tesla [facts 10–18]. *(Evidence-supported)*

**Key conclusion.** Value migrates from merchant / status-quo cell supply constrained relative to mass-market EV volume toward Tesla-led mega-scale, co-located precursor-to-pack manufacturing capacity — if and when the plan is built and partners are formalized. *(Reasoned Inference, Medium)*

**Tier 1 is assigned; magnitude is not established.** Tesla occupies the position: it owns the mass-market vehicle demand forecast, discloses the Gigafactory integration plan, states the pack $/kWh reduction target, and expects to fund about $2 billion of the $4–5 billion CapEx [facts 1, 5, 10, 17, 18]. Tesla also states it has no experience in lithium-ion cell production and that cell-production partnerships are not yet formalized [facts 21, 22]. The position is therefore plan-ownership and demand-aggregation, not demonstrated cell-manufacturing control. Share of economic profit retained is **Unknown**. *(Reasoned Inference for the position, Medium; Unknown for the share)*

**Top companies for further research — not recommendations, not ranked by attractiveness.**

- **Tesla Motors, Inc.** — discloses and intends to lead the Gigafactory plan; NASDAQ-listed registrant. Tier 1; share retained Unknown. *(Reasoned Inference, Medium)*
- **Panasonic Corporation** — named as Tesla’s present lithium-ion cell supplier under an amended supply agreement for Model S / Model X needs; not named as a Gigafactory production partner at T0. Existing supplier; Gigafactory role Unknown. *(Evidence-supported for current supply role; Unknown for Gigafactory role)*

Market blind spot: treating the announcement as a real-estate, jobs, or CapEx spectacle while underweighting battery pack $/kWh as the binding constraint on a mass-market EV. *(Reasoned Inference, Medium)*

## 3. Customer Problem

**What end-user problem changed.** The binding problem Tesla states is battery cost (and, for Gen III volume, battery supply) relative to a mass-market electric vehicle. Source A frames projected lithium-ion demand as an opportunity to reduce cost faster than previously thought possible, and ties a greater-than-30% pack $/kWh reduction to the first year of mass-market volume production [facts 1, 5]. Source C states Gen III is intended at a lower price point and higher volumes than Model S, and that Tesla will need a high-volume supply of lithium-ion cells at reasonable prices [facts 14, 17, 21–23]. *(Evidence-supported)*

Two components are distinguishable:

- **Pack cost per kWh as the product constraint.** Tesla’s explicit >30% pack $/kWh target is the quantified customer-facing cost lever [facts 5, 17]. *(Evidence-supported)*
- **Long-term cell supply scale.** Near-term cells are covered by the Panasonic amendment; long-term / Gen III needs may exceed that path without additional sources or Gigafactory production [facts 20, 21, 23]. *(Evidence-supported)*

**Who is worse off without it:** Tesla’s intended Gen III / mass-market buyers if pack cost and cell volume remain at status quo — implied by Tesla’s linkage of Gigafactory cost reduction to Gen III volume production and by the risk that reliance on others for Gen III cells could constrain profitable pricing [facts 1, 5, 17, 23]. Absolute willingness-to-pay, prior pack cost, and addressable volume are not disclosed. Magnitude of the customer problem is **Unknown**.

## 4. Industry Change

**The concrete change.** Tesla discloses a shift from status-quo cell procurement toward a planned mega-scale factory that co-locates battery precursor material, cell, module, and pack production with strategic battery manufacturing partners / suppliers [facts 2, 4, 10]. *(Evidence-supported)*

**Which system requirement shifts.**

1. **From merchant cell purchase toward integrated precursor-to-pack production.** The Gigafactory is defined as integrating those stages in one location [fact 10]. *(Evidence-supported)*
2. **From status-quo cell-cost trajectory toward faster cost decline via scale and co-location.** Tesla states design intent to reduce cell costs much faster than the status quo and to exceed 2013 worldwide lithium-ion annual production by 2020 [fact 4]. *(Evidence-supported as Tesla’s claim)*
3. **From near-term contracted cell supply toward a Gen III high-volume path that may require new sources.** The Panasonic path is stated for the next few years; Gen III long-term needs may require additional suppliers or Gigafactory production [facts 20–23]. *(Evidence-supported)*

**Why the prior solution becomes insufficient.** Tesla does not claim current packs cannot be built; it claims mass-market / Gen III economics require faster pack $/kWh reduction and high-volume cells at reasonable prices, and that Gigafactory delay would force reliance on others and could constrain Gen III profitability [facts 1, 5, 17, 21, 23]. *(Reasoned Inference)*

**Structural or cyclical.** Structural if executed: participants must change what they do — Tesla and unnamed partners build co-located cell/pack capacity; Gen III pack supply is planned from the Gigafactory rather than solely from merchant purchase [facts 2, 10, 14]. Execution is early-stage and partnerships are unformalized [fact 22], so realization remains contingent. *(Reasoned Inference)*

## 6. Value Migration

**Where value moves — origin and destination both named.**

| From (origin layer) | To (destination layer) | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lithium-ion cell manufacturing (merchant / status quo) | Lithium-ion cell manufacturing (Gigafactory co-located) | Facts 2, 4, 10, 21–23 |
| Separated precursor / cell / module / pack process chain with logistics waste | Integrated precursor-to-pack co-location at one Gigafactory site | Facts 2, 10 |
| Pack $/kWh absorbing mass-market EV price room | Pack cost reduction (>30% /kWh target) enabling Gen III volume economics | Facts 5, 17 |
| Near-term contracted merchant cell path alone for long-horizon volume | Tesla-led CapEx plus partner production capacity for Gen III packs | Facts 14, 18, 20–22 |

**Why the migration follows.**

1. Tesla states mass-market / Gen III requires lower pack $/kWh and high-volume cells at reasonable prices [facts 1, 5, 17, 21]. *(Evidence-supported)*
2. Tesla states the status-quo cell-cost path is insufficient relative to the Gigafactory design intent [fact 4]. *(Evidence-supported as claim)*
3. Tesla proposes integrating precursor, cell, module, and pack in one factory with partners, funded partly by Tesla CapEx [facts 2, 10, 18]. *(Evidence-supported)*
4. Therefore value migrates toward mega-scale integrated cell/pack manufacturing capacity under Tesla’s plan, and away from reliance on status-quo merchant cell supply for the Gen III horizon — if the factory is built and partnerships formalize. *(Reasoned Inference)*

**Links not established.** Magnitude of profit migration is **Unknown** (no current pack $/kWh, no Gen III price, no partner economics). Site, design, and partnerships are explicitly early-stage [fact 22]. *(Unknown)*

**Confidence on the migration as a whole: Medium.** *(Reasoned Inference)* Direction is well supported by Tesla’s own problem, remedy, and CapEx statements. Realization, partner identity, and magnitude are open. Under `VALUE_CAPTURE_METHODOLOGY.md` §6, undisclosed load-bearing elements cap confidence below High.

**Statement of standing.** Potential value reallocation. Requires later market verification.

## 7. Value Capture

Per `VALUE_CAPTURE_METHODOLOGY.md`: value creation is not value capture. A >30% pack $/kWh reduction and 2020 production scale are *creation* claims [facts 4, 5, 17]; the question is who retains surplus. Every conclusion below carries exactly one evidence label and one confidence level.

**Step 1 — Industry bottleneck.** High-volume lithium-ion cells and pack $/kWh at levels that allow a mass-market / Gen III EV to be produced and sold profitably [facts 1, 5, 17, 21, 23]. *(Reasoned Inference, Medium)*

**Step 2 — Scarce capability.** At T0, the scarce disclosed capability is Tesla’s combination of (a) projected Gen III / mass-market vehicle demand that can underwrite mega-scale battery CapEx and (b) a stated plan to co-locate precursor-to-pack production — not Tesla cell-manufacturing know-how, which Tesla states it does not have [facts 1, 2, 10, 18, 22]. *(Reasoned Inference, Medium)*

**Step 3 — Value migration.** As in §6: toward Gigafactory co-located cell/pack capacity; away from status-quo merchant cell dependence for the Gen III horizon.

**Step 4 — Economic profit capture.** No current pack cost, Gen III ASP, partner margin split, or JV ownership is disclosed. CapEx totals are stated without return figures [fact 18]. Magnitude of capture is **Unknown**. *(Unknown, Low)*

**Step 5 — Ranking.**

### Tier 1 — Direct Value Capturer

**Tesla Motors, Inc.** *(Reasoned Inference, Medium)*

Retention mechanism, in three parts:

1. **Demand aggregation and product ownership.** Tesla states the mass-market / Gen III vehicle as the demand that justifies leveraging lithium-ion volume and Gigafactory pack supply [facts 1, 14, 17]. A merchant cell maker alone does not own that vehicle program on these facts. *(Reasoned Inference, Medium)*
2. **Plan and CapEx control of the integration path.** Tesla discloses the Gigafactory concept, the >30% pack $/kWh target, and about $2 billion of expected CapEx out of $4–5 billion [facts 2, 5, 10, 17, 18]. *(Reasoned Inference, Medium)*
3. **Pack and vehicle integration.** Tesla already manufactures packs at the Tesla Factory and intends Gen III vehicle production there, with Gigafactory packs feeding that path [facts 14, 16]. *(Reasoned Inference, Medium)*

**Counter-evidence recorded.** Tesla has no cell-production experience; partnerships are unformalized; site and factory design are incomplete; Tesla itself states CapEx and timeline could exceed expectations and that failure would force reliance on others for Gen III cells [facts 22, 23]. Cost reductions could be competed into Gen III price. These keep confidence at Medium and magnitude at Unknown. *(Reasoned Inference, Medium)*

**Magnitude of capture: Unknown.** *(Unknown, Low)*

### Tier 2 — Direct Beneficiary

**Vacant.** *(Reasoned Inference, Medium)* Production partners would be the natural Tier 2 candidates on CapEx-sharing and cell know-how [facts 2, 13, 18, 22], but no partner is named and Tesla states partnerships are not formalized. Naming a firm would be an inferred participant and is forbidden. Panasonic’s existing supply role is merchant purchase for Model S/X, not a disclosed Gigafactory retention mechanism [fact 20].

### Tier 3 — Indirect Beneficiary

**None assigned.** Stationary storage applications are named as Gigafactory demand [facts 14, 15] without a named storage customer or retention path. *(Unknown, Low)*

### Negatively affected / displaced (not a beneficiary tier)

**Merchant / status-quo cell supply for Gen III long-horizon volume** *(Reasoned Inference, Low)* Tesla’s plan substitutes co-located Gigafactory cell production for sole reliance on additional merchant sources for Gen III [facts 4, 10, 21–23]. Panasonic is the named present supplier [fact 20]; whether Gigafactory displaces Panasonic volume, expands it, or enlists Panasonic as a partner is **Unknown**. No company-wide Panasonic impact is established.

### Tier 4 — Potential Beneficiary

- **Unnamed strategic battery manufacturing / production partners** *(Hypothesis, Low)* Intended co-operators [facts 2, 13, 22]; identity and terms Unknown.
- **Panasonic Corporation** *(Unknown, Low)* Could become a Gigafactory partner, remain a merchant supplier, or lose Gen III volume share; none of these outcomes is stated at T0 [facts 20–22].
- **Mass-market EV customers** *(Hypothesis, Low)* Stated pack $/kWh reduction may accrue as consumer surplus via Gen III pricing [facts 5, 17]. Not excluded.
- **Unnamed precursor / raw-material suppliers** *(Unknown, Low)* Integration of precursor material is stated [fact 10]; participants and pricing Unknown.

**Alternative explanations.** That partner cell makers capture most manufacturing rent while Tesla only finances demand — possible given Tesla’s stated lack of cell experience [fact 22] *(Hypothesis, Low)*. That Gen III never reaches volume and Gigafactory CapEx destroys value — possible on Tesla’s own risk language [fact 23] *(Hypothesis, Low)*. That status-quo merchant supply meets Gen III needs without Gigafactory — Tesla treats this as a constrained path [facts 21, 23] *(Reasoned Inference, Low)*.

## 9. Risks

### Assumptions that could fail

1. **Gigafactory is built on a timeline that serves first-year Gen III / mass-market volume** — supports §§4–7 and Tier 1 mechanisms 1–2. Timeline and “approximately three years” / 2020 full capacity are Tesla forward-looking statements [facts 1, 14, 15]. *(Unknown at T0)*
2. **>30% pack $/kWh reduction materializes** — supports §§3, 6, and creation claims behind capture. Figure is Tesla’s expectation [facts 5, 17]. *(Unknown)*
3. **Cell-production partners with significant experience are formalized on terms that leave Tesla retaining disproportionate surplus** — supports Tier 1 vs partner-capture alternative. Tesla states partnerships are not formalized and that it lacks cell experience [fact 22]. *(Hypothesis)*
4. **Demand for Gen III / mass-market and stationary storage underwrites the $4–5B CapEx** — supports migration scale [facts 15, 18]. Volume and pricing are undisclosed. *(Unknown)*
5. **Site selection among AZ / NV / NM / TX and factory design complete without CapEx or schedule blowout** — supports realization [facts 11, 12, 22, 23]. *(Unknown)*
6. **Panasonic’s merchant role does not become the enduring Gen III cell path that renders Gigafactory unnecessary** — supports migration away from status-quo merchant cells [facts 20, 21, 23]. *(Hypothesis)*

### What would weaken the thesis

- Gigafactory CapEx or operating cost exceeds the $4–5B / expectations band without matching cost-per-kWh progress.
- Pack $/kWh reduction materializes below the >30% target in the first year of Gen III volume.
- Partner terms assign most manufacturing rent to production partners.
- Gen III pricing passes most pack-cost savings to customers.
- Site, design, or partnership delays push pack supply past the stated Gen III window.

### What would invalidate the thesis

- **Tesla abandoning the Gigafactory** or failing to bring cell/pack production online such that Gen III remains dependent on merchant cells without the integrated cost path [fact 23].
- **Economic profit from lower pack $/kWh accruing primarily to formalized production partners**, such that Tesla’s role is demand financing without retention.
- **Gen III / mass-market vehicle failing commercially**, so the demand aggregation that underwrites mega-scale battery CapEx does not exist.
- **Status-quo merchant cell supply meeting Gen III volume at pack $/kWh levels that remove the need for co-located Gigafactory production**, reversing the migration.

Weakening and invalidating conditions are kept separate deliberately: the first set reduces the size of the conclusion; the last set replaces it.
